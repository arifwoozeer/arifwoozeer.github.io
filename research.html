Research

I’ve become increasingly interested in relational AI, particularly how people come to trust and turn to conversational systems for support, and what that might mean for care and human relationships.

Much of this interest grew out of my experiences with peer support and community research in Singapore. I keep coming back to questions about what makes support useful to the person receiving it, and how their circumstances shape what they need. As conversational AI becomes part of how people seek advice and emotional support, I’m interested in how these systems respond to those circumstances, especially when the options available to someone are limited.

OTR Copilot

Affective Computing capstone · Over The Rainbow

OTR Copilot grew out of my work with Over The Rainbow, where I developed an AI tool to assist peer mentors in supporting people who sought emotional support. The prototype combined response suggestions with tools for reviewing emotional patterns across conversations, and I tested it with five OTR mentors using fictional conversation scenarios.

I explored how mentors could inspect and question the tool’s suggestions while drawing on their own understanding of the people they support. Testing showed that real-time suggestions could compete for attention during a conversation, while reviewing patterns across sessions helped mentors reflect on what someone had shared over time.

I presented the work as a digital poster at the UCL Behaviour Change Conference and will present related research as a poster at AMIA 2026 in Dallas.

Emotional support when professional care is difficult to access

Independent research · 2026–present

I’m currently developing a study on how conversational AI responds when someone seeks emotional support but faces barriers to professional care. These might include the cost of counselling, concerns about privacy, or a reluctance to disclose personal information. I’m interested in whether models account for these circumstances when offering suggestions, and whether they continue to address the concern that brought someone to the conversation.

The planned experiment uses fictional conversations set in Singapore, varying both the person’s circumstances and when the model learns about them. In some conversations, the relevant information is available from the outset. In others, the person reveals it as the conversation progresses. By comparing the responses, I hope to understand how models adjust their suggestions as they learn more about the person’s situation, particularly when the usual routes to professional support may be difficult to pursue.

The study follows from questions I encountered during OTR Copilot about the assumptions underlying AI-generated advice and how those assumptions affect its usefulness to someone seeking support.

Multilingual conversational AI and migrant worker financial literacy

Reach Alliance · Singapore

Through the Reach Alliance, we examined how migrant workers in Singapore used conversational AI to understand financial information across languages. The project explored how workers made sense of the responses they received, where language created difficulties, and how these experiences shaped their ability to navigate financial decisions.

We conducted the research as part of a broader effort to understand the challenges migrant workers face when accessing financial information and support, and documented the work in a Reach Alliance case study.
