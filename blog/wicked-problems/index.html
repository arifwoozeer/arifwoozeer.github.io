Wicked Problems

August 2026 8 min read

A wicked problem is a complex social or cultural challenge that is difficult or impossible to solve because of incomplete, contradictory, and changing requirements. [1]

The term was coined by design theorists Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber in 1973. The word "wicked" does not mean evil; it means resistant to resolution. [1][2]

For most of my university life, nearly every encounter with a friend, peer, or acquaintance would somehow start with "how's Kura?" The question became a kind of reflex greeting … a check-in that also manifested itself in a form of accountability.

Kura wound down over Summer 2025 but I've been putting off writing this for a while for many reasons… Where do I start? Where do I end? Did Kura fail? Did I fail?

But as I looked through the photo album Arin and I created, (to create the photo collage seen above), the emotions that arose, wistfulness/gratitude/a sense of relief?), I'm somewhat at ease that maybe failure isn't the right framing? (though it might be a remnant of some residual delusion, a separate topic for another day…)

KURA KURA PRIVATE LIMITED started after National Service. I essentially got a first-hand view of what could be described as the last-mile "delivery gap" in youth mental health. Veric, Roydon and I conceived of it as a response to that gap, specifically in the context of COVID-19. What began as a rough idea became a social messaging board, then a dashboard, then a fully fledged app. Eventually it was Arin and I taking it to the finish line (or as far as we could take it).

Over five years: 2,500 users across Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia. Over S$70,000 raised. Nearly 20 people through the team at various points, some unpaid in the early days. Trips to Ho Chi Minh City, Graz, conferences I would have never imagined myself participating in as a university student. A lot of pitching. A lot of grant reports. A lot of 11pm Slack messages.

I can emphatically say that being an "entrepreneur" was never the motivation.. the mission to making the act and process of journaling easier was the primary goal, which, as it turns out, makes it harder to figure out when to stop.

On measurement

So did it actually help anyone? John Doerr channels the philosophy of Intel's Andy Grove: a metric must leave no room for argument in his book Measure What Matters. At the end of a quarter, you must be able to look at a metric and answer with a simple "yes" or "no" as to whether it was achieved. If it is subjective or relies on judgment, it is a bad key result.

The problem is that mental health outcomes are hard to measure, and the academic literature on digital health tools is full of studies that use engagement (session frequency, retention, streaks) as proxies for wellbeing. In digital mental health, pre- and post-use surveys are a common way of assessing whether a tool works. But that opens another can of worms. What framework are you using? How big is the sample? And does asking someone to fill out a 30-question survey before and after using an app really tell you whether the app helped?

Martin Seligman's PERMA framework offers one lens: wellbeing involves positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, accomplishment. A journaling app might touch one or two of these (on a good day). If someone journals every day but their relationships are deteriorating, have you helped them? Conversely, if someone has a poor mood but is able to describe their emotions and the associated contributing factors, can that be deduced as a sign that the app has assisted in translating their feelings into words?

In the literature there is a reference to a phenomenon known as Goodhart's Law. When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. For us, that meant daily active users became the goal (which meant streaks, gamification, push notifications, the whole engagement playbook). [Nudge by Nir Eyal energy] All of it optimised for retention rather than change. We weren't necessarily unique in this but applying that heuristic to a mental health app always felt disingenuous.

This chasm felt impossible to reconcile. Was it fair to the user to design a UX flow that would leave them "hooked"? We were convinced that working towards a positive utility meant that it was fair game but was there merit to trying to optimize for retention when retention may not correlate with better overall mental health outcomes? Even randomized controlled trials fail to really capture and isolate the effect of any particular system or app in material behavioral changes in one's mental health. A 2024 study co-authored by Duolingo's own learning scientists found that users' likelihood of continued engagement had no significant relationship to their actual language gains.

The same problem came up with our advisors, grant facilitators and partners. When building a dashboard for secondary school students, there was a lot of discourse (rightly so) on what metric would be ideal for teachers to track. Persistent "low" moods? Length of journal entries? Potential for self harm? The permutations felt endless in trying to map it out. I'm cognizant that there probably isn't one right answer here but I do wonder how many people in the mental health tech scene are convinced that one exists.

On first principles

I remember being asked at a conference in Graz: why an app?

I gave the standard tech blue pilled answer : scalability!! At the time to me the question almost didn't make sense. My internal dialogue was obstinate, "bro what is this guy on? Can't they see that in order to deliver impact you need a medium that can propagate across the masses??" I was convinced that an app was THE panacea that could be that in between for users caught up in a support system that was heavily stigmatized. There was merit to the argument but we always came back to the product. The roadmap. Move fast break things. Lean startup cycle. What the next feature would look like. We never really (or rarely did) stop to ask whether tech was the right intervention at all.

Apparently, there's a term for this. Evgeny Morozov, a writer who has spent years writing about technology and politics, calls it technological solutionism: treating social problems as if they just need a better piece of technology. A pothole-reporting app is a simple example. It can make reporting easier, but it doesn't fix why the potholes aren't being repaired in the first place. It made me wonder how often we do the same thing with bigger problems: build a tool because building a tool feels easier than dealing with the problem underneath it.

But alas at this point i wanna give myself or at least the team some grace. We had grant disbursements tethered to specific tech outcomes. I was in a computing school. We were in the post covid tech rush, people were very much convinced that given the success of analogous solutions in education/productivity/wellness people were passing around case studies of Duolingo, Calm and Headspace, that it could translate to mental health as well.

There's nothing wrong with building tech solutions and the era of vibe coding has accelerated that trend, there's effectively no barrier to building a fully decked out web app/saas tool. But the process of interrogating whether tech should be the solution was one that we never really scoped out/ I wonder if that's something that the mental health tech community could coalesce around and figure out.

Mental health in young people is shaped by housing, employment, family dynamics, stigma, access to care, and economic precarity, among other things, and a journaling app sits very far downstream of all of that. Looking back, I don't think the problem was that we didn't care about the mission but that we didn't properly interrogate the tools we were bringing to it..

Being around people who shared the same assumptions made them feel like facts. So we built, iterated, and kept going without really asking whether we were building on the right foundation.

On what running a company actually means

Running Kura also taught me things that are hard to understand from the outside.

Once you are responsible for payroll, people's livelihoods are no longer an abstract part of running a business. You know that the salary you are paying someone matters to their life outside work. You also see what happens after people leave. One former team member moved on to another company, and we like to think some of the skills and experience they gained at Kura helped them get there. The same was true for others. Nearly 20 people passed through Kura and got some early-stage experience that helped them take their next step.

It also changed how I think about the private sector. I came into Kura with a social enterprise lens and some fairly strong reservations about what companies are for, particularly when it comes to "public interest" areas like mental health technology, and how commercial incentives can sit uneasily alongside social needs.

In many ways i still hold many of these concerns (would like to ventilate these at some point) but running one made me see that a company is also a group of people whose incomes, skills and opportunities are tied to the decisions being made. It doesn't resolve the concerns I had, but it made it harder to see the private sector as simply "profit bad."

Closing

So, did Kura fail? I'm not sure that's the right question.

We built an app to address a difficult problem, with limited resources and changing circumstances. Most of the team was still in university, and we were figuring a lot of it out as we went. There are decisions I'd make differently now. There are others I'd make the same way.

What I can say is that we took it as far as we could, and eventually decided to stop.

P.S. / Epilogue?

I don't think I'm done with the questions Kura raised. I'm still interested in what responsible AI in mental health support should look like, but I don't think I'll be the person building the next product. I'd rather ask the people who are: what are they responsible for, what should policy look like, and where are the limits of what technology can actually do?

That's part of what pulled me towards research. I'm now working independently on how AI systems behave in therapeutic conversations: the assumptions they make, and what happens when those assumptions are challenged.

Reach Alliance has kept some of those questions alive too. Working with people thinking about impact in very different settings has made me more interested in a question I keep coming back to: when we say something works, what are we actually claiming, and what evidence do we have for it?

Kura was where I started asking some of these questions. I don't think it'll be where I stop.
